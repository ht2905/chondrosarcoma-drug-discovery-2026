ABSTRACT: Molecular mechanics/Poisson−Boltzmann (GeneralizedBorn) surface area is one of the most popular methods to estimate
binding free energies. This method has been proven to balance accuracy
and computational eﬃciency, especially when dealing with large systems.
As a result of its popularity, several programs have been developed for
performing MM/PB(GB)SA calculations within the GROMACS
community. These programs, however, present several limitations. Here
we present gmx_MMPBSA, a new tool to perform end-state free energy
calculations from GROMACS molecular dynamics trajectories.
gmx_MMPBSA provides the user with several options, including binding
free energy calculations with diﬀerent solvation models (PB, GB, or 3DRISM), stability calculations, computational alanine scanning, entropy
corrections, and binding free energy decomposition. Noteworthy, several
promising methodologies to calculate relative binding free energies such
as alanine scanning with variable dielectric constant and interaction entropy have also been implemented in gmx_MMPBSA. Two
additional toolsgmx_MMPBSA_test and gmx_MMPBSA_anahave been integrated within gmx_MMPBSA to improve its
usability. Multiple illustrating examples can be accessed through gmx_MMPBSA_test, while gmx_MMPBSA_ana provides fast, easy,
and eﬃcient access to diﬀerent graphics plotted from gmx_MMPBSA output ﬁles. The latest version (v1.4.3, 26/05/2021) is
available free of charge (documentation, test ﬁles, and tutorials included) at https://github.com/Valdes-Tresanco-MS/gmx_
MMPBSA.

INTRODUCTION
Molecular mechanics/Poisson−Boltzmann (GeneralizedBorn) Surface Area (MM/PB(GB)SA) has become one of
the most popular methods to estimate binding free energies
since its introduction in the late 1990s.1,2 This method
achieves a good balance between accuracy and computational
eﬃciency, being more accurate than most scoring functions,
especially when dealing with protein−protein and protein−
nucleic acid systems, and less computationally demanding than
alchemical free energy methods. MM/PB(GB)SA is a highly
versatile method that allows estimating binding free energies,
determining structural stability, predicting hotspots, and
assessing the contributions from individual residues or energy
terms by free energy decomposition analyses.3−5
Several computational tools used to perform MM/PB(GB)SA calculations have been developed and released over the
years. MMPBSA.py is a Python script implemented in the
Amber package that allows users to quickly and eﬃciently
calculate free energies from molecular dynamics (MD) and
Monte Carlo simulations.6,7 MMPBSA.py has been the leading
tool for performing MM/PB(GB)SA calculations within the
Amber package due to its improved accessibility, implementation, and capabilities as compared to older scripts like
mmpbsa.pl.6 Multiple implicit solvation models are available

within MMPBSA.py, including the PB, GB, and 3D-Reference
Interaction Site Model (3D-RISM). Normal mode or quasiharmonic analysis can be used to calculate vibrational

interactions can be analyzed using alanine scanning or free
energy decomposition. All these procedures can be speeded up
by dividing frames evenly across available processors, thus
providing the users with an eﬃcient and user-friendly program
with the ﬂexibility to accommodate most of their needs.6,7
On the other hand, several toolkits have been developed to
conduct MM/PBSA calculations on structural ensembles
derived from GROMACS8 trajectories. g_mmpbsa implements
the MM/PBSA approach using subroutines written in-house or
sourced from the GROMACS and APBS9 packages. It is
distributed as a stand-alone tool with a user interface similar to
those of other GROMACS modules. This tool provides a

Here, we present a new tool named gmx_MMPBSA
designed to ﬁll the gap and bring AmberTools’s rich
functionalities from MMPBSA.py and associated programs to
GROMACS users. Several calculations can be performed
within gmx_MMPBSA, including but not limited to binding
free energy calculations with diﬀerent solvation models (PB,
GB, or 3D-RISM), stability calculations, computational alanine
scanning, entropy corrections, and binding free energy
decomposition. Two additional tools have been integrated
within gmx_MMPBSA: gmx_MMPBSA_test and
gmx_MMPBSA_ana. gmx_MMPBSA_test provides the user
with multiple illustrating examples (https://valdes-tresancoms.github.io/gmx_MMPBSA/examples) and allows whether
gmx_MMPBSA was installed successfully to be checked.
gmx_MMPBSA_ana, on the other hand, is a graphical user
interface that provides fast, easy, and eﬃcient access to
diﬀerent graphics plotted from gmx_MMPBSA output ﬁles.
The gmx_MMPBSA suite has been written in Python 3.8 and
uses the MMPBSA.py calculation engine, a widely used tool for
streamlining end-state free energy calculations. The program is
freely available (documentation, test ﬁles, and tutorials
included) at https://github.com/Valdes-Tresanco-MS/gmx_
MMPBSA.

binding free energies of noncovalently bound complexes
(Figure 1).

Furthermore, the entropic component is usually calculated
by normal modes analysis (NMODE). The translational and
rotational entropies can be estimated using standard statistical
mechanical formulas. Nevertheless, calculating vibrational
entropy using normal modes is computationally expensive
because it requires expanding the internal coordinate
covariance matrix for all degrees of freedom for a set of
minimized structures.15 Conversely, the Quasi-harmonic (QH)
approximation is less computationally expensive, although it
requires a considerable number of frames to converge.16
Recently, other alternatives have been developed, such as
NMODE in truncated systems, which considerably reduces the
computational cost.17 Interaction Entropy (IE) is another
novel method that calculates the entropic component of the
binding free energy directly from MD simulations without any
extra computational cost. This method is numerically reliable,
more computationally eﬃcient, and superior to the standard
NMODE approach, as shown in an extensive study of over a
dozen randomly selected protein−ligand binding systems.18
Typically, two approaches are used for MM/PB(GB)SA
calculations, known as Single Trajectory Protocol (STP) and
Multiple Trajectory Protocol (MTP).5,12,19 In STP, both the
receptor and the ligand trajectories are extracted from that of
the complex. This approach is valid when the bound and
unbound states of the receptor and the ligand are similar. It is
computationally less expensive than the MTP approach since
only a simulation of the complex is required. Additionally, the
potential internal terms (e.g., bonds, angles, and dihedrals)
cancel exactly in STP since these terms are the same in both
bound and unbound states.5,12 On the other hand, the MTP is
a more realistic approach because it considers multiple
trajectories (i.e., complex, receptor, and ligand). However,
signiﬁcant conformational changes can lead to numerous
errors.5,12,20 In practice, a detailed study of the system is
required to select the approach to be used.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
gmx_MMPBSA is written in Python 3.8 and combines
functions from GROMACS8 and AmberTools12 to generate
input ﬁles in a consistent/reproducible manner and perform
end-state free energy calculations. We will ﬁrst illustrate the
general workﬂow and how all the tools are integrated into the
gmx_MMPBSA environment. Next, we describe the diﬀerent
tools (i.e., gmx_MMPBSA_ana and gmx_MMPBSA_test) that
have been built to improve gmx_MMPBSA usability. Later, we
include a thorough comparison of gmx_MMPBSA vs other
programs widely used within the GROMACS community to
perform MM/PBSA calculations. Finally, we present a set of
illustrative examples to demonstrate the versatility and
potential applications of gmx_MMPBSA.
Workﬂow. gmx_MMPBSA divides work into three major
steps: (i) preparation, (ii) calculation, and (iii) visualization
and analysis (Figure 2).
i. Preparation. The MMPSA.py calculation engine is used
within gmx_MMPBSA to perform most of the calculations.
The preparation process is focused on generating the
topologies in Amber format in a consistent and reproducible
way. In this step, the PDB structure ﬁles for the receptor and
ligand are generated from the GROMACS structure+mass(db)
ﬁles (i.e., *.tpr, *.pdb, or *.gro). Of note, GROMACS diﬀers in
several aspects from Amber in terms of data structure. Amber
renumbers residues starting from 1, while GROMACS is more
ﬂexible, allowing the same residue numbers in diﬀerent chains

MMPBSA.py is one of the most popular and complete
software packages for performing MM/PB(GB)SA calculations. This tool is released with AmberTools, and as such, its
engine requires Amber *.prmtop topology ﬁles as input.
Several tutorials describe how to generate Amber topologies
from GROMACS ﬁles; however, they are cumbersome and
multistep processes, leading to errors. Within gmx_MMPBSA,
we have optimized this process to handle large and
heterogeneous systems. Additionally, we have included several
Amber-compatible force ﬁelds (i.e., zwitterionic amino acids22)
to ensure compatibility with as many systems as possible. A
comprehensive list of the diﬀerent systems tested so far can be
found at https://valdes-tresanco-ms.github.io/gmx_
MMPBSA/examples.
Unlike MMPBSA.py, which uses an additional tool (anteMMPBSA.py) to generate the topologies for the STP
approximation and change the atomic radii, gmx_MMPBSA
performs the process internally. gmx_MMPBSA separates the
receptor and ligand in the preparation step, avoiding the
requirement for additional procedures or external applications.
Similarly, our tool generates the alanine mutant without an
external tool or a user-deﬁned topology. This reduces unforced
errors and speeds up the input ﬁle preparation process.
ii. Calculation. Multiple calculations (e.g., binding free
energy with diﬀerent solvation models (PB, GB, or 3D-RISM),
stability, alanine scanning, entropy corrections, and binding
free energy decomposition) can be conducted with
gmx_MMPBSA. The preparation and execution of alanine
scanning and energy decomposition have been substantially
facilitated as compared to their implementation in
MMPBSA.py. As a new facility, interaction entropy calculations can now be conducted. For these new features, several
variables in the input ﬁle have been added or modiﬁed in
gmx_MMPBSA. A comprehensive description of the added/
modiﬁed variables can be found at https://valdes-tresanco-ms.
github.io/gmx_MMPBSA/input_ﬁle.
iii. Visualization and Analysis of Results. Once the
calculation is ﬁnished, a graphical user interface application
(gmx_MMPBSA_ana) shows up to visualize and analyze the
results. A detailed description of gmx_MMPBSA_ana
functionalities is provided below.
gmx_MMPBSA Tools. gmx_MMPBSA includes three
tools: gmx_MMPBSA, which constitutes the application
core; gmx_MMPBSA_ana, a tool for visualizing and analyzing

the results; and gmx_MMPBSA_test, which helps users to
familiarize themselves with the application, as well as inspect a
set of provided examples.
gmx_MMPBSA. gmx_MMPBSA carries out the ﬁrst two
steps (i.e., preparation and calculation) described in the
workﬂow. Additionally, it has an application programming
interface (API) that provides direct access to the raw data
produced during the calculation (https://valdes-tresanco-ms.
github.io/gmx_MMPBSA/summary/#gmx_mmpbsa-in-anutshell).
gmx_MMPBSA_ana. gmx_MMPBSA_ana is a graphical
user interface (GUI) tool that has been built with the Python
PyQt5 library for analyzing gmx_MMPBSA results. This tool
provides a clean and user-friendly work environment using a
multidocument interface that eases the graph analysis process,
either individually or in sets. gmx_MMPBSA_ana has a
multiprocessing capacity for reading and loading the results,
thus reducing the processing time considerably. It uses
seaborn23 and matplotlib24 for plotting, as well as pandas25
and numpy 26 for manipulating the data. Currently,
gmx_MMPBSA_ana allows analyzing multiple systems simultaneously and computing the correlation between calculated
and experimental energies in a user-deﬁned set. Several graphs
can be plotted in gmx_MMPBSA_ana, for instance, per-frame
line and average bar graphs for each energy component,
temporal heatmaps for per-residue free energies, relational
heatmaps for per-wise free energies, and linear regression
graphs for correlation analyses between the systems. Of note,
each chart has a toolbar that allows editing several features in
the graphs and exporting high-quality images. A thorough
review of all kinds of graphs supported in gmx_MMPBSA_ana
can be found at https://valdes-tresanco-ms.github.io/gmx_
MMPBSA/analyzer.
gmx_MMPBSA_test. We have currently provided
gmx_MMPBSA users with a set of examples that show how
the program works and the variety of systems and analyses that
gmx_MMPBSA supports. Users can access them directly from
the repository or by using gmx_MMPBSA_test. This tool
downloads the current version of the example folder from the
repository and uses exactly the command line recommended in
each example. The process is carried out using multiprocessing,
which allows running the examples in less time. Once all the
calculations are ﬁnished, gmx_MMPBSA_ana is launched to
display the results of all the selected systems. In this way, we
delivered a tool to test gmx_MMPBSA operation and
familiarize the user with the characteristics, methods, and
analyses available in our application (https://valdes-tresancoms.github.io/gmx_MMPBSA/command-line/#running-gmx_
mmpbsa_test).

gmx_MMPBSA vs Other Programs. Hereunder, we
compare gmx_MMPBSA with the diﬀerent existing GROMACS programs to perform MM/PBSA calculations. The
comparison includes g_mmpbsa,10 a tool widely used within
the GROMACS community, and GMXPBSA2.1.11 We have
also considered MMPBSA.py in the comparison, as it
constitutes the base for gmx_MMPBSA.
Calculation Features. gmx_MMPBSA includes the GB, PB,
and 3D-RISM models for estimating the solvation energy
during binding free energy or stability calculations. Thus far,
no program allows GROMACS users to perform calculations
using diﬀerent GB models. The previously licensed Sander
code was recently released, thus enabling unrestricted use of
the GBHCT,27 GBOBC, GBOBC2,28 GB-Neck,29 and GB-Neck2
models.30 These models are widely used in free energy
calculations as they allow achieving good results at a reasonable
computational speed. In addition, part of the system can be
treated by quantum mechanics using a hybrid quantum
mechanical−molecular mechanical (QM/MM) Hamiltonian
and a GB model, thus allowing a more accurate description of
the system (Table 1). The model given by Pellegrini and
Field31 is used in the QM/MM generalized Born implementation, with QM/MM PME support based on the work by
Walker et al.32
Furthermore, several ﬁnite-diﬀerence numerical solvers are
available through gmx_MMPBSA, such as linear PB
solvers,33,34 which can be used for standard applications such
as modeling of globular proteins.35 On the other hand,
nonlinear PB solvers36 are recommended when dealing with
highly charged systems, such as nucleic acids. An implicit
membrane region with a uniform37 or heterogeneous38
dielectric constant can also be considered in the calculations
(Table 1).39 Neither g_mmpbsa nor GMXPBSA2.1 has this
feature implemented despite the APBS capability to simulate a
membrane environment.40
gmx_MMPBSA also incorporates 3D-RISM for calculating
solvation energies (Table 1). 3D-RISM is based on ﬁrstprinciples and gives a comprehensive molecular description of
the solvation.41,42 It considers various interactions between the
solvent and the solute (e.g., hydrophobic interactions,
hydrogen bonds), making it the most rigorous statistical
mechanics solvation model available in gmx_MMPBSA.
Nevertheless, it is prohibitive for several applications due to
the large memory and CPU time required for the calculations.

Furthermore, gmx_MMPBSA implements a new method for
estimating the entropic term IE method, in addition to the two
(NMODE and QH) already available in MMPBSA.py. The
main advantage of this new method is its low computational
cost, making it attractive for high throughput energy
calculations.43−45 Neither g_mmpbsa nor GMXPBSA2.1 has
any of these methods implemented in their routines. Although
several studies conclude that correlations based on eﬀective
energy may be suﬃcient,3,46 others assert that entropic
correction can improve these estimates.43,47,48 In any case,
we provide the user with diﬀerent methods to estimate the
entropic component that can be executed independently
(Table 1).
Although the GB and PB models are not strictly
decomposable, the per-wise scheme can provide information
about pairwise interactions between residues. Similarly, both
alanine scanning and per-residue decomposition allow
estimating the contribution of a particular selection of residues.
Currently, g_mmpbsa only supports residue decomposition,
while GMXPBSA2.1 only supports alanine mutation at several
residues simultaneously. These analyses shed light on the
receptor−ligand interaction, though the results of a per-residue
or per-wise decomposition should be interpreted carefully.
gmx_MMPBSA implements a more ﬂexible selection method
than MMPBSA.py when selecting residues from the complex
to print. The interacting elements in the receptor and the
ligand within a certain distance can be spotted by using the
“within” keyword in gmx_MMPBSA (see an example at
https://valdes-tresanco-ms.github.io/gmx_MMPBSA/
examples/Decomposition_analysis). This reduces considerably
both the computation time and the amount of generated raw
data, as well as errors when listing the residues (Table 1).
Finally, gmx_MMPBSA implements both the STP and MTP
approach. The user can decide which approach to use based on
the system’s behavior during the production run. Only
GMXPBSA2.1 allows comparison between diﬀerent systems
(e.g., comparing a mutant and the wild type), which will be
implemented soon in gmx_MMPBSA. Additionally,
gmx_MMPBSA allows deﬁning several trajectories of the
same system, allowing the analysis of multiple replicas in the
same calculation without concatenating them previously
(Table 1).

Technical Features. g_mmpbsa and GMXPBSA2.1 present
some compatibility issues with modern GROMACS versions
(20xx.x), while gmx_MMPBSA can use any GROMACS
version included in the PATH to extract the structures,
generate the indexes, and clean up the trajectories. We have
successfully tested several GROMACS versions, including but
not limited to 4.6.5, 5.1.2, 2018.3, 2019.1, 2020.3, and 2021.1
(Table 3).
All the programs can run in parallel, although there are
diﬀerences in their execution and implementation. g_mmpbsa
depends exclusively on the APBS parallelization; thus, it is
necessary to conﬁgure both the external use of APBS and
environment variables that control the number of processors to
use. Conversely, GMXPBSA2.1 takes advantage of both locally
available processors and PBS/Torque queuing in a cluster.
Both gmx_MMPBSA and MMPBSA.py natively incorporate
parallelization with Message Passing Interface (MPI) through
the mpi4py library.50 That way, it eﬃciently leverages either
processors available locally or on multiple nodes in highperformance computing (HPC) systems. In addition, it does
not require any additional process, execution of external
applications, or user intervention (Table 3).
The calculation and analyses are performed as a single-step
execution process within gmx_MMPBSA. On the contrary,
several python scripts are required in g_mmpbsa to perform
the ﬁnal statistical analyses and calculate the energetic
contributions of each residue in the complex. Likewise,
GMXPBSA2.1 comprises a suite of Bash/Perl scripts for
streamlining MM/PBSA calculations (Table 3).

Comparing QM-MM/GBSA vs MM/PB(GB)SA + IE Method.
Lectin RSL from Ralstonia solanacearum complexed with Meα-L-Fucose was obtained from the X-ray crystal structure
(PDB ID: 2BT9)57 and used as a study case to evaluate the
performance of QM-MM/GBSA vs MM/PB(GB)SA + IE

Figure 5A shows Me-α-L-Fucose bound to the intermonomeric site of Lectin RSL. Noteworthy, W31 establishes a
CH···π stacking interaction with Me-α-L-Fucose.57 This
interaction between an aromatic amino acid and the hydrophobic face of the carbohydrate can contribute up to 5 kcal/
mol.58 Figure 5B shows a comparison among diﬀerent
methods to calculate the binding free energy. In this particular
case, the PB model performed better than the GB model.
Furthermore, the addition of the entropic component
improves the calculations (ΔGcalc = −12.7 ± 2.5 kcal/mol
with PB; ΔGcalc = −25.2 ± 2.1 kcal/mol with GB), although
the binding free energy value calculated with the GB model is
still far from the experimental one (ΔGexp = −8.6 kcal/mol).
On the other hand, QM-MM/GBSA outperformed (ΔGcalc =
−9.5 ± 1.5 kcal/mol) both classical approaches, in agreement
with previous ﬁndings.59 QM-MM/GBSA success relies on
using a DFTB Hamiltonian, which better describes the
interactions between the active site residues and Me-α-LFucose, particularly the dispersion eﬀects.
